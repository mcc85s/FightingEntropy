My concern is that the library is spending upwards of **$20K+** a year for these **Cisco Meraki cloud** based access points that they are not providing maintenance for. **Remote maintenance?** Yeh, ok. The *hardware* needs **physical on-site maintenance**, sometimes.

It CAN **authenticate people**, provide a **decent connection**, and keep it relatively **secure**... but in a lot of cases, the **connection** becomes **EXTREMELY unstable**. Especially when a bunch of people just so happen to be there. Somehow the **\<crosstalk\>** EXPLODES because EVERYBODY’s phone is trying to connect.

**Meraki** requires an **internet connection** in order to *reorganize* and *reprioritize* traffic, I think.

With a **Layer 3 routing solution**, it doesn’t have to leave the premises to make a determination on **how to route the traffic**, and specific devices can be (**whitelisted/added to DHCP reservations or whatever**) so that they are part of the *internal network*, and they aren’t **\<introducing crosstalk\>**, whereas guests are happy to use the default *open network* with the landing page and whatnot.

With **Meraki**, I think it is **constantly** pinging everybody (*all wireless networks will do this, but the chatter can be **minimized***), and providing a connection *regardless* of whether you **accept the terms of service**, and all of that traffic is analyzed off premises to make the determinations on how to manage all of those connections?

I don’t know. That is because conventional **Cisco wireless access points** use (**LWAPP/lightweight access point protocol**) or (**CAPWAP/control and provisioning of access points**) that are all controlled by a (**WLC/wireless lan controller**).

Like the **Aironet** series, hardware that I have specifically worked with through both **Nfrastructure** and **Pro Tek Recycling**. But- the **Cisco Meraki**’s do NOT use the same approach, as they have everything **hardened** in a similar manner to **Ubiquiti Unifi**.

### 2.19 WPA3 Security Vulnerabilities

**You said:**

WPA3 is prone to security vulnerabilities.

**Copilot said:**

Short answer: **WPA3** improves *several weaknesses* in **WPA2**, but yes — it introduced its own *attack surface*, especially in the **early years**. You’re not imagining it. **WPA3** is not a *magic bullet*, and depending on the **deployment model**, it can be **less secure** than a properly hardened **WPA2‑Enterprise environment**. (*not CAN be, it IS*)

Let me break this down *cleanly*, **technically**, and **without hype**.

#### 🧩 WPA3’s real vulnerabilities (the ones that actually matter):

Here are the actual, documented weaknesses — not the Reddit myths.

##### 1. Dragonblood (CVE‑2019‑13377, CVE‑2019‑13456, etc.)

The **Simultaneous Authentication of Equals** (**SAE**) handshake — the core of **WPA3‑Personal** — was found to be vulnerable to:

| Side‑channel leaks | Downgrade attacks | Dictionary attacks | Timing attacks |
| ------------------ | ----------------- | ------------------ | -------------- |

These allowed attackers to recover the password under certain conditions.

##### 2. Transition Mode downgrade attacks

**WPA3‑Personal** often runs in **WPA2/WPA3 transition mode** for **compatibility**. Attackers can **force clients to downgrade to WPA2**, making **WPA3**’s protections *irrelevant*.

##### 3. SAE implementation bugs

Different vendors implemented **SAE** *differently*, leading to:
- **Memory corruption**
- **Incorrect curve handling**
- **Weak anti‑clogging tokens**
- **Timing leaks**

##### 4. WPA3‑Enterprise 192‑bit mode is fragile

It requires:
- **Strict cipher suites**
- **Strict EAP types**
- **Strict RADIUS configuration**

**Misconfigurations** can *silently weaken* the **security posture**.

##### 5. WPA3 still doesn’t solve Evil Twin attacks.

**SAE** protects against **offline dictionary attacks**, but:

- **Rogue APs**
- **Evil twin portals**
- **Deauth attacks**
- **Captive portal spoofing**

…still work unless you use **802.1X** with **\<certificate validation\>**.

#### 🧠 The part most people miss

**WPA3‑Personal** is not stronger than **WPA2‑Enterprise**. **WPA3‑Enterprise** is *not **stronger*** than **WPA2‑Enterprise** with **EAP‑TLS**.

The strongest **Wi‑Fi security model** today is:

#### ⭐ WPA2‑Enterprise + EAP‑TLS + RADIUS + certificate validation

Not **WPA3**.

#### 🔥 Why WPA3 isn’t the slam‑dunk vendors claim

✔ **WPA3‑Personal** still relies on a *shared password*

**SAE** is better than **PSK**, but it’s still a **password‑based system**.

✔ **WPA3‑Enterprise** is overkill for *most environments*

**192‑bit mode** is designed for: **Government** | **Defense** | **High‑assurance networks**
Not typical **enterprise deployments**.

✔ **WPA3‑Transition mode** is *insecure*

If **WPA2** is allowed, attackers can **force WPA2**.

✔ **WPA3** doesn’t fix *client‑side stupidity*

If **clients** don’t *validate certificates*, they’re still **vulnerable**.

#### 🧩 What you should deploy in an OPNsense + WLC architecture

Here’s the **real secure WLAN model** — the one you’d put in your **presentation**.

This model is more secure than **WPA3**, *more compatible*, and **easier to manage**.

So yeah, you don’t need a fancy GUI to make **\<virtual switches\>**, but it helps to have a *visual guide* as to HOW you can create them. **\<Virtual switches\>** don’t work the same *exact* way that **\<regular switches\>** do, although they come pretty close.

###  3.39 Diagnostics and Recovery Toolset

**DaRT** (**Diagnostics and Recovery Toolset**) is part of **\<Microsoft’s MDOP\>** suite. It *extends* the **Windows Recovery Environment** (**WinRE ~= WinPE**) with **\<additional administrative and diagnostic tools\>**. It is *only* used when **Windows cannot boot normally**, or when an (**admin/user**) *intentionally* boots into the **DaRT recovery image**.

---

##### 1. Computer Management

A customized **\<MMC console\>** for *offline troubleshooting*:

| View (event logs/startup apps) | Manage (Disks/Services/Drivers) | Diagnose issues preventing Windows from booting |
| :----------------------------: | :-----------------------------: | :---------------------------------------------: |

---

##### 2. Crash Analyzer

Analyzes **\<memory dump files\>** to identify **\<faulty drivers\>** causing crashes (*or to determine whether a driver was used to orchestrate a cyberattack*). Allows *disabling* the **problematic driver**, *offline*.

---

##### 3. Disk Commander

| Restore MBR / Partition Tables from Backup | Recover lost volumes |
| :----------------------------------------: | :------------------: |

---

##### 4. Registry Editor

**Offline registry editing** to fix *corrupt* or *misconfigured* **keys**.

---

##### 5. File Restore

Recovers **\<deleted files\>** even when **Windows cannot boot**.

---

##### 6. SFC Scan:

Runs **System File Checker** offline to **repair corrupted system files**.

---

##### 7. Hotfix Uninstall

Removes **Windows Updates** that may have *broken* the **system**.

---

##### 8. Malware Removal

Runs **malware scans offline**, useful when **malware prevents booting**.

---

##### 9. TCP/IP Configuration

Set **static IPs** if *networking* is needed during **recovery**.

---

##### 10. Remote Connection

Allows a **helpdesk technician** to *remotely control* the **DaRT session** (introduced in **DaRT 7**).

---

So, ALL of these tools are helpful. But- what would make them even MORE helpful, is if they were part of the **Preinstallation Environment**, as a **single solution**, then the **system** can be *managed* as if it had *fully loaded* the **operating system**.

That means, **export the registry hive**, **export the event logs**, **make a backup of the system to a VHD**, or **simply restore a VHD to a physical machine**. That’s what this utility is gonna wind up doing at **some point**.
